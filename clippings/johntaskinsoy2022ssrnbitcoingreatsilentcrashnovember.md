# The Great Silent Crash of the 21st Century*

Dr. John Taskinsoy ${}^{a}$

## ABSTRACT

A mysterious creator under the alias Satoshi Nakamoto (a pseudonym) launched the world's first successful cryptocurrency in early January 2009 which, not only was a historic moment, but was one that cultivated a technology revolution and money's evolution into a digital form. However, technical issues (inherent flaws) in the design of Bitcoin blockchain, non-technical issues (political backlash, regulatory hurdle, and environmental hazard), plus the opaqueness surrounding the launch of Bitcoin have opened the door for an endless debate, incessant criticism, spurious claims, heated arguments, plethora of articles, and media frenzy contemplating what Bitcoin really is (crypto-asset, commodity, or investment vehicle) or it is not (currency). On the technical side, blockchain that made Bitcoin a household name fails miserably; high latency (8 minutes or more), low transaction throughput (7 per second), low-scalability (mining, proof-of-work based validation using consensus and cryptography), and high energy cost make Bitcoin unfit to compete with new and fast-scalable cryptocurrencies such as Solana's transaction speed (50,000 per second) or XML's $0.00001 fee per transaction. Although technical problems are not without a solution, non-technical issues are not easy to resolve because their resolution depends on politicians, law makers, regulators, and various government agencies (central banks, the Fed and the ECB in particular) who choose to run headlong into backlash to Bitcoin and other cryptocurrencies. On Tuesday (October 8, 2022), prices of cryptocurrencies tanked, citing the industry-shaking collapse of FTX (second largest after Binance), some even dubbed the event as "Crypto's Lehman moment". But erratic price movements is not something new in the crypto industry which has been on a roller-coaster since December of 2021, i.e. after Bitcoin price hit almost $68,000 and its market cap $1.24 trillion, jittery investors in a hurry began to cash out their hefty gains. The inability of FTX's CEO Sam Bankman-Fried to handle his plan to sell his company (which was regarded as one of crypto's "blue chip" companies) to the rival crypto exchange Binance set off a widespread selling panic, as a result, cryptocurrency market shed a mindboggling $236.7 billion ($81 billion by Bitcoin) in just two days (Tuesday and Wednesday), which by any standard was insanely bonkers.

# 1.0 Introduction

Ever since money was invented¹, economies where coinage or paper money was used as a medium of exchange have become increasingly susceptible to shocks in form of economic, financial and currency crises (for the history of money, see Davies, 2002). Before the invention of money, there was bartering which dates back to the prehistoric people; it is a form of trade without the use of a monetary medium such as metallic (silver, gold, copper) money, paper (fiat) money, plastic money (credit, debit, store, gas), digital coin or electronic cash (Bitcoin) to facilitate exchange. A bartering system both in theory and practice is distinctly different than a monetary-based economy; for instance, a barter transaction is extremely simple, executed unilaterally or multilaterally without the need of a trusted third party intermediation (i.e. banks, credit cards, and no transaction costs). Also in barter economy, only goods or services are used for exchange of other goods and services; moreover, immediate delivery takes place at the conclusion of a mutual agreement. Unlike the merciless capitalism where exploitation of labor supersedes social welfare, barter is a purely peer-to-peer system of exchange, and this aspect of bartering makes it free of the capitalist system’s complex problems and its inflationary monetary mechanisms. Under barter, agents of the economy are not concerned with frictional costs, asymmetry of information, economies of scale, exploitation of labor, and forms of employment. More importantly, the barter system does not revolve around expanding or contracting of the aggregate money supply through credit (printing and unprinting money), therefore there is no debt hangover.

Although the probability of a high-magnitude economic or financial crisis to occur is circa 5% (once every 20 years or so, about every 10 years in the U.S.), the recurrence of contemporaneous modern crises is nothing of new, they had occurred in the past but more disruptive and longer-lasting crises promise to strike in the future. Despite the world undergoing several hundred cases of financial folly (Reinhart & Rogoff, 2009, see Table 1²), the human race is still very much incapable of recognizing warning signs (writings on the wall) of the next crisis, eradicating causes, or predicting in advance the time of a future crisis. Douglas and Wildavsky (1983) asked; “Can we know the risks we face, now or in the future? No, we cannot: but yes, we must act as if we do”. Banks, an inextricable part of any economy, facilitate and allocate real and scarce resources from savers to investors who enter into intertemporal contracts through space and time amid uncertainties. Therefore, banks not only have an endless propensity to adopt financial innovation to cope with asymmetry of information, imperfect frictions and transaction costs (Arrow, 1964), but a normal-functioning banking system can provide signs of stability or instability and be a good predictor of future prospects (Levine, 1997)³.

# 1.1 The Silent Crash

The birth of Bitcoin in January 2009 (first successful cryptocurrency), as its dramatic crash in recent months, was also silent; it was just a phenomenon among some computer engineers and enthusiasts. It is mindboggling that, to this day, the creator of a digital coin (electronic cash) whose market cap ${ }^{10}$ has passed $\$ 1$ trillion ($\$ 1.28$ trillion on 8 November 2021) is still anonymous. In the crypto-currency literature, it is commonly found that Bitcoin was invented by a mysterious creator under the alias Satoshi Nakamoto (a pseudonym), but the opaqueness surrounding the launch of Bitcoin has opened the door for an endless debate, incessant criticism, spurious claims, heated arguments, plethora of articles, and media frenzy contemplating what Bitcoin really is. Satoshi Nakamoto (2008) in his white paper entitled *Bitcoin: A Peer-to-Peer Electronic Cash System* explains it as a secure, permissionless (decentralized) network of electronic cash that runs on blockchain as its infrastructure technology using a distributed ledger and cryptography. Bitcoin is a mineable cryptocurrency, divisible by eight decimals and the smallest denomination is called a Satoshi (i.e. $0.00000001 \text{ BTC}$). Unlike gold (the densest, conductor, malleable and ductile metal), Bitcoin has no intrinsic value, no physical form (only exists digitally), and is stateless (i.e. no de facto Fed-like central authority to set its value).

The dollar’s century-long hegemony ('exorbitant privilege') underpinned by American imperialism has prompted a search for alternative currencies (i.e. Bitcoin). Many historians argue that imperialism is in America’s DNA; in fact, the evolution of American imperialism and the dollar’s hegemony are tightly intertwined. The seeds of an imperial nation were sowed by the European settlers arriving in the new world after 1492, thrived with blood via the use of Monroe Doctrine with “manifest destiny” which made the US territorial expansion throughout the Americas justified and inevitable (Dunning, 2001; McDonough, 2011). The Revolutionary War (1775-1783) was a defining moment in US history, igniting the spark of American imperialism; but the U.S. was poles apart from its humble beginnings, the U.S. expansionism via intervention and annexation became a tradition with winning the Spanish-American War of 1898, the treaty of Paris (December 10, 1898) ended the Spanish colonial rule in Americas and resulted in the U.S. acquisition of new territories in western Pacific and Latin America such as Puerto Rico, Philippines, and Guam (e.g. Corbin & Levitsky, 2003). The emergence of the U.S. as an imperial power made her crave more, the next was winning insular possessions in the Caribbean and Hawaii and playing a leading role in the restructuring of power in Europe (i.e. power was shared by Germany, France, and the UK which were also each other’s chief enemy). In the post-WWII, while Europe has moved towards “perpetual peace”, the U.S. on the other hand has turned into an anarchic Hobbesian backed by its military might, weaponized dollar, and the abuse of sanction power.

Although the U.S. dollar has enjoyed its hegemonic status (exorbitant privilege) for more than half a century, Bitcoin supporters began to believe that the dollar's days were numbered and Bitcoin (highly secure, permissionless – decentralized network of electronic cash) could actually become a viable alternative to the U.S. dollar (Cermak, 2017).

According to Franco (2014), Bitcoin may never become a real currency for everyday use because special knowledge is prerequisite to gain an understanding of three elements about Bitcoin; software (cryptography), hardware (computer network), and the underlying infrastructure technology (blockchain). Yermack (2013) contends that Bitcoin is a currency, while Berentsen and Schär (2018) argue that Bitcoin has applications suitable to become an important asset class, but not a currency. It is true that currently Bitcoin is not used in everyday transactions or accepted by everyone like fiat money; however, the count of merchants and nations accepting Bitcoin as a currency is increasing rather fast. Bitcoin serves as a store of value (for the main drivers of Bitcoin price, see Kristoufek, 2015); unlike fiat currencies, Bitcoin is a decentralized crypto (or digital) currency, therefore its value is not set by a central bank (i.e. Fed). # High-Latency of Bitcoin Blockchain

By design, high latency (inherent flaw) is an Achilles heel in Bitcoin blockchain; on average, verifying (i.e. conforming and validating) transactions takes around 8 minutes (low of 5 and high of 20 minutes) which does not resonate with requirements of the modern financial system and makes Bitcoin unfit to compete against other very fast-scalable cryptocurrencies (covered in the previous section) that also have extremely low latency. The latency problem was not a software bug that has surfaced after Bitcoin was launched, it was in fact the proof-of-work (PoW) that Satoshi Nakamoto made it central (heart) of Bitcoin design (Figure 27) where the level of difficulty of the hashing algorithm is constantly adjusted so that a new block can only be created every 10 minutes. Unfortunately, Bitcoin’s very high latency cannot be resolved without making foundational changes to its blockchain; for instance, one solution may be moving away from both hideous and time-consuming PoW consensus approach; another solution could be taking the validation process off the blockchain so that Bitcoin’s TPS can be improved significantly, nevertheless these solution may still not be enough to compete with the transaction speed of Solana (50,000 TPS) or XML’s average transaction fee of $0.00001.
